# Questions

(Or: A Carrier Bag for Conceptual / Philosophical threads I would like to explore.)[^*]

## Complexity

1. Does all complexity arise from simplicity? Is that which looks random (to us) simply just something generated from a short program we haven't found yet. Is the world algorithmic or random?[^1]
2. Are some things irreducible? Do some things have no smaller form? Chaitin's Omega says yes: some truths have no simpler cause at all; they're true for no reason. Which parts of this tile could I explain, and which can only be watched?[^2]
3. Complexity as the computer sees it vs as the eye sees it. The animation runs forever, and every couple of seconds the computer nudges a handful of numbers. To the computer, almost nothing happens. To us, things are created, born, and constantly changing. Is this emergence, and where does it happen: in the program, or in us?[^3]
4. Bounded evolution: is there such a thing? Right now each number is held near home on purpose. But a finite program can evolve without limit: Chaitin modelled life as a program that keeps mutating, and it keeps getting fitter forever. What would this tile become if nothing ever called it home, and would it still be the same piece?[^4]
5. Automata. A cellular automaton is the purest version of this idea: a grid, one tiny rule, and a picture that unfolds forever. Rule 110 has just eight cases, yet it can compute anything. My tile isn't one: a shader has no memory, and each frame forgets the last. What would change if each frame grew out of the one before?[^5]

## Compression and understanding

6. What is the shortest program that draws this tile, and is that length a property of the tile itself? The counter at the bottom only gives an upper bound. There may always be a shorter program, and no method can prove there isn't.[^6]
7. Is understanding compression? Chaitin says a theory is good when it's much shorter than what it explains. Repetition is the simplest compression there is: see one tile, and you know them all. So why are we so pleased by it? Maybe the pleasure is in the moment of getting it, which would explain why a pattern that changes slowly keeps us watching. What does that say about information theory, and the ways people use it to explain art?[^7]
8. What is the significance of informationally dense units? In this program a single number, the hue or the zoom, reshapes the whole mosaic at once. A Forgotten Languages story imagines an alien message written in the Collatz sequence because it is "superior in compactness, robustness, and information density." Is density what makes a unit feel meaningful, or just powerful?[^8]
9. Is a pattern in the world, or in the eye that sees it? We find patterns even where there are none, and more so when we feel out of control. "You cannot in fact conceive of an unorganized structure." If that's true, is every pattern partly our doing?[^9]
10. Or neither? Object-oriented ontology says a thing can't be reduced to its parts (circles, rectangles, numbers) or to its effects (what we see). Is the tile an object in its own right, withdrawn from both the code and the eye?[^10]

## Seeing

11. Is there a unique processing power to the human eye? We take in the gestalt in a way computer vision can't. But maybe the question isn't whether machines see like us. Parisi argues that neural nets already make percepts that are "exclusively algorithmic rather than optical." What would a genuinely alien way of seeing patterns look like, and is this tile already made for one?[^11]

## Making

12. The computer as intermediary: the digital interpenetrates the work just as it interpenetrates our modern reality. I wrote the rules, the program draws every frame, and you, watching, decide which moments are events. Is authorship lost when we use an intermediary? Or is it distributed, like everything else these days?[^12]
13. How does one design the ideal small piece? Small in bytes, and small in idea: one triangle, one rule, one gesture. Is the ideal the shortest program, or the shortest program that still surprises its author?[^13]
14. Alchemy! Isn't this *solve et coagula* in action? I took inspiration from a real-life pattern block, extracted its essence, broke it down into simpler pieces (triangles, rectangles and circles), and built a system that recombines them into infinite new forms. Alchemical mandalas did something similar: a whole cosmology folded into one symmetric diagram.[^14]
15. The fundamental shape is asymmetric by definition: if it weren't, you could reduce it further (QED). Curie said asymmetry is what creates the phenomenon. The reflections supply the symmetry, and everything interesting lives in how the triangle breaks it. Are certain asymmetries "better": more alive once reflected?[^15]
16. Shaders and parallelism: a shader is a pure function, f(space, time) → color, worked out for every pixel at once, with no memory and no order. Elegance and simplicity, form and function. Because the whole image answers instantly to one number, a digital artist can be "gestural" with their proverbial paint strokes: the gesture is a number moving.[^16]

[^2]: Wolfram calls a process “computationally irreducible” when there's no shortcut to its result: you have to run every step. S. Wolfram, *A New Kind of Science* (2002), ch. 12.
    Chaitin's Omega goes further. Its digits are mathematical facts that are true for no reason: no program shorter than the digits themselves can produce them, and no simpler axioms imply them. G. J. Chaitin, “Randomness and Mathematical Proof,” *Scientific American* 232(5) (1975); *Meta Math! The Quest for Omega* (2005).

[^3]: Each change is tiny in the space of numbers but large in the space of images. The mutations on this page are a small example of what M. Bedau calls “weak emergence”: behaviour that follows from the rules but can only be found by running them. “Weak Emergence,” *Philosophical Perspectives* 11 (1997).

[^4]: Right now the change is bounded on purpose: positions stay within 6 units of their start, sizes within 3, and each change has a 35% chance of sending a number home. Open-ended evolution is listed as a central open problem in M. A. Bedau et al., “Open Problems in Artificial Life,” *Artificial Life* 6(4) (2000).
    From the bibliography of Forgotten Languages' [“Postwork Civilizations and Games”](https://forgottenlanguages-full.forgottenlanguages.org/2018/02/postwork-civilizations-and-games.html) (2018): G. J. Chaitin, *Proving Darwin: Making Biology Mathematical* (2012), and “Life as Evolving Software,” in H. Zenil (ed.), *A Computable Universe* (World Scientific, 2012), 277–302. Chaitin's “metabiology” treats an organism as a program and mutation as a random walk through the space of programs. Fitness is how large a number the program can name, a version of the Busy Beaver problem, so there is no ceiling: the organism can always get fitter. Later work showed the model meets a formal definition of open-ended evolution.

[^10]: Object-oriented ontology (Graham Harman, Levi Bryant, Ian Bogost, Timothy Morton) holds that a thing is never exhausted by what it's made of or by what it does. Explaining it away by its parts Harman calls “undermining”; explaining it away by its effects or appearances, “overmining.” G. Harman, *Object-Oriented Ontology: A New Theory of Everything* (2018).
    Timothy Morton takes this into art: “the aesthetic dimension is the causal dimension” (*Realist Magic: Objects, Ontology, Causality*, 2013). For Morton, causing something is a kind of appearing. Here that's literal: when a number glides, the only thing it can cause is a change in how the tile looks.
